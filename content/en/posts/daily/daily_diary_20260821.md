*Written August 21, 2026*

Since I started studying Agent Engineering, I've realized something about what I work on and what the core abilities of my job as a developer really are. When I first got the job, I thought writing "easy-to-read" code was what it required. But that turns out to be only part of the picture. Since developers work at the interface between people and computers, their main job inherently includes communication. They have to solve the problems their employers want solved, so problem structuring and problem resolution must be part of it too.

The size of the company matters here. Few small companies have dedicated roles for company-wide service divisions like DBAs, server engineers, or infrastructure engineers. So their developers have to become experts in all of this knowledge — at least within their company's service scope. This turns developers into perpetual students. They study every day, ask questions in communities like Stack Overflow, and discuss with other developers to solve problems that someone else may have already solved. I think this is what exhausts most developers who haven't yet adapted to the job.

---

But now, they have a powerful tool to build these abilities: **the LLM agent.** It can discuss topics whenever you want, search for new tech stacks, help you understand them easily, and analyze logs that are too long to read manually. This lets you catch up on tech stacks and apply them to your job — DevOps, automation, and more. Of course, there are issues like the fear of job substitution by AI, FOMO, and companies being slow to shift their workflows to AI-friendly ones. But I think, eventually, somehow, this will become our work environment.

So we should get ready for it. AI can make the work that developers do with computers dramatically faster than ever before. Then our main work will be connecting people to computers *through* AI. As I said, at that point we need to communicate with our companions, create easily understandable problem structures from the problems we get in meetings, and express them logically and specifically — so that both our companions and AI can understand.

Developers will have to talk and think about problems more than they let AI do it for them. They have to play the role of the **final harness** — the one who stops AI from doing work it wasn't authorized to do.

Last of all, here's the thing: **be a chatterbox, developer.**
